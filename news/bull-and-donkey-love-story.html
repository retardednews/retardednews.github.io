Scientists Went to Ethiopia to Study Monkeys. Over Lunch, They Spotted a Couple Nobody Saw Coming

The pair have different owners, different species and plenty of other options. They keep choosing each other anyway, and now they're in a scientific journal.

By RetardedNews Staff · November 2, 2026 · Ethiopian highlands, Ethiopia · 3 min read

A team from Italy's University of Pisa travelled to an Ethiopian plateau to watch geladas, the grass-eating monkeys with a bright red patch on their chests. During a lunch break, their attention wandered to a nearby pasture, where something much stranger was going on.

A bull was mounting a donkey. A male donkey. And the donkey did not seem to mind one bit.

The researchers were so surprised they briefly wondered whether they had imagined it. They had not. So they switched into science mode and started taking notes.

Meet Achille and Patroclo

The team named the pair Achille and Patroclo, after Achilles and Patroclus, the famously close heroes of Greek legend. Between March 6 and March 19, 2026, the researchers counted 51 mountings. The donkey often invited them, and instead of kicking or fleeing, he nipped playfully at the bull.

It was not just about sex. The two licked, rubbed and nuzzled each other, and spent their days side by side, even though cows, donkeys of both sexes and other cattle were all around them. When Achille tried mounting a different male donkey, that donkey flattened his ears and made it very clear he was not interested.

"We believe the key finding is not simply that a bull mounted a male donkey." (lead author Martina Francesconi)

The scientists, who published the case in the journal Current Biology on November 2, say the point is that the behaviour was part of a choosy, lasting social bond, not a case of mistaken identity or a lack of options. Earlier reports of animals mating across species have mostly involved coercion or confusion.

The animals belong to different owners and only met in the shared pasture. Sadly, the owners reportedly separated them after the researchers left. For now, the greatest bromance in the history of the Ethiopian highlands lives on mainly in a peer-reviewed paper.
