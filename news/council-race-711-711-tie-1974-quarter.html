A City Council Election Ends 711 to 711. The Winner Is Decided by a 52-Year-Old Coin

Minnesota law says a tie must be settled "by lot." Both candidates were allowed to inspect the coin first. Only one got to call it.

By RetardedNews Staff · November 13, 2026 · Pine Island, Minnesota, USA · 2 min read

Pine Island, a small city about 20 minutes north of Rochester, Minnesota, had two city council seats on the ballot on November 3. The first was settled the normal way. The second refused to be.

When every vote was counted, incumbent Delana Weis and challenger Dominick Pahl had exactly the same total: 711 votes each.

Minnesota has a rule for that. When an election ends in a tie, state law tells the local canvassing board to decide the result by lot, which in plain English means a game of chance. Pine Island went for the classic option.

Heads or tails

On Thursday evening the city held a special meeting at City Hall. Both candidates were allowed to inspect the coin before it went into the air, to make sure there was nothing funny about it. As the incumbent, Weis got to call it.

She chose heads. The coin, a quarter minted in 1974, landed heads up. Weis keeps her seat.

Pahl took it well. He congratulated his rival, said he was glad Weis would be doing the job and told reporters he plans to run again.

There is a strange legal wrinkle. Under Minnesota rules, a recount can only happen after the losing side asks for one, which means the coin had to pick a winner first. Pahl could still request one, but by his own account he is happy to let the quarter have the final word.
