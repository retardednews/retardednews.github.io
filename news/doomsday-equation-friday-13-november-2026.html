In 1960, Scientists Worked Out the Exact Day Humanity Would End: Friday, November 13, 2026

The paper ran in one of the world's top science journals, and for decades its numbers were scarily accurate. Then something changed.

By RetardedNews Staff · November 13, 2026 · Urbana, Illinois, USA · 3 min read

In November 1960 the journal Science printed a paper with one of the most alarming titles in its history: "Doomsday: Friday, 13 November, A.D. 2026". It was written by the physicist Heinz von Foerster and two of his students, Patricia Mora and Lawrence Amiot.

Their argument was about people, not bombs or asteroids. The trio gathered 24 estimates of the world's population stretching back roughly 2,000 years and noticed that the time it took for humanity to double in number kept getting shorter.

Most living things grow, at best, exponentially. Humans, the authors argued, were different: because we talk, trade and team up, more people meant more cooperation, and more cooperation meant even faster growth. Run that curve forward and the doubling time hits zero. The population heads for infinity. They even put a date on it.

"Our great-great-grandchildren will not starve. They will be squeezed to death." — von Foerster, Mora and Amiot, Science, 1960

Demographers and mathematicians lined up to write in and call the work ridiculous. The authors defended their math. The idea spread anyway, into textbooks, newspapers, a Canadian parliamentary committee and even a comic strip. The paper also cracked a dark joke about shipping surplus people into space with no return ticket.

The awkward part: it kept working

For years the real numbers stayed embarrassingly close to the doomsday curve, closer than many professional forecasts in the 1970s and 1980s. Only around 1993, roughly halfway to the big day, did the world's actual population start falling behind.

The reason is the twist. The paper's own suggested escape route was to cut the global birth rate in half. Today the birth rate is a little under half of what it was in 1960. In other words, humanity more or less followed the prescription.

There were clues that von Foerster was not entirely serious. Before science he had worked as a magician, and 13 November happened to be his birthday. Researchers today see the paper less as a prophecy than as a clever way to point at a startling pattern. The date itself finally arrived on Friday, November 13 with rumours swirling on social media. In Vienna, a science centre marked it with a conference on the sense and nonsense of end-of-the-world predictions. The world, for the record, is still here.
