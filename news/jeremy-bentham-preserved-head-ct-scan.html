A Philosopher's Real Head Has Been Locked in a University Safe for Almost 200 Years. Scientists Just Scanned It

His skeleton sits in a glass case, dressed in his own clothes. The head on top is wax. The real one finally got a check-up, and it explained a lot.

By RetardedNews Staff · November 13, 2026 · London, England, UK · 3 min read

Jeremy Bentham, the English thinker behind utilitarianism, had unusual plans for his body. His will asked for it to be dissected in public for science and then preserved and displayed. He even coined a word for the result: an auto-icon, or self-image.

After he died in June 1832, his friend Thomas Southwood Smith did as asked. Bentham's skeleton was padded out, dressed in his own clothes and seated in a wooden cabinet. It can still be visited at University College London today.

The head was the problem. Smith tried to preserve the real one, but the result was so grim that he ordered a wax head to sit on the body instead. The genuine article now lives in a secure conservation safe.

What the scanner saw

To mark UCL's 200th anniversary, a team from the university's collections, its Hawkes Institute and its medical physics department ran the fragile head through a CT scanner. It was too big to fit the machine's field of view in one go, so they imaged the front and back separately and stitched the two halves together, without ever handling it.

The scans showed teeth in a dreadful state and a badly damaged lower jaw, with signs of arthritis. In other words, one of history's great thinkers about pleasure and pain spent years with crippling toothache.

In an 1830 letter, Bentham wrote that he had more than once "submitted to the momentary torment to rid myself of the enduring one."

The scan also revealed a hole in the top of the skull where a metal rod once fixed the head to the body, a few traces of the tissue around the brain, which was removed during preservation, and a pair of beautifully crafted glass eyes, complete with anatomically correct optic nerves.

The team has printed a 3D copy of the skull and hopes to share the data so teachers can print their own. A Bentham expert at UCL said the man who donated his body to science would almost certainly have approved.
