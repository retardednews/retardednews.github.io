Scientists Wire Up Monkeys' Brains and Sit Them Down for a Movie Night. The Film They Chose Is Perfect

A serious neuroscience study about how we make sense of what we see needed a test film. The macaques got cartoon chaos, in two languages.

By RetardedNews Staff · November 14, 2026 · City College of New York, New York, USA · 3 min read

Watching a film is supposed to be the moment your brain finally clocks off. A new study in the Journal of Neuroscience suggests your brain never got that memo, and it used some unusual moviegoers to prove it.

A team led by Vinay Raghavan at the City College of New York wanted to know how primates pull meaning out of a scene as their eyes dart around it. We don't take in the world in one smooth sweep. Our eyes jump from spot to spot, pausing briefly on each, and the question was what the brain does with all those little glimpses.

The audience

First, dozens of people wore EEG caps on their scalps and watched ordinary feature films, including Whiplash and The Big Sick. For a closer look, the team also recorded from electrodes placed inside the brain, in a separate group of human patients and in two female rhesus macaques.

Those deeper recordings needed shorter clips. So the monkeys, sitting in for science, watched ten-minute chunks of Despicable Me. One clip was in English. Another was in Hungarian.

They also watched a short animated film called The Present and three five-minute nature documentaries starring, naturally, other macaques.

What the brains revealed

The researchers tracked where the viewers looked and matched that with brain activity. They found that when a glimpse brought in genuinely new information about the scene, which they call semantic novelty, it changed activity well beyond the brain's basic visual areas, including regions tied to attention and scene processing. Some of that signal appeared early and fast, between one fixation and the next, as if the brain were predicting what it was about to see.

Their conclusion is that vision works less like a slideshow of separate snapshots and more like a continuous story the brain keeps updating. Raghavan suggested the finding might even help people building AI vision systems.

What it cannot tell us, as 404 Media's Becky Ferreira pointed out, is what a macaque actually thinks of the Minions. Or whether they found them funnier in Hungarian.
